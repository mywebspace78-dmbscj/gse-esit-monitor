Introduction

One of the great debates on Wall Street is how the GSEs, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, will finally be resolved: the terms set before their release from conservatorship, the release itself, their relisting on a major stock exchange and a return to fair market value. The GSEs were chartered as government-sponsored enterprises to provide liquidity to the mortgage market, purchase mortgages and retain the credit risk. They were two of the most successful and profitable financial services stocks from the 1980s through 2007. Eighteen years ago, during the financial crisis, the government rescued the GSEs with extensive funding to keep them operating and prevent a systemic failure. Then, as today, FNMA and FMCC financed over 50% of the residential housing market. Under conservatorship, the companies have once again become extremely profitable, paid significant dividends to the government and repaired their balance sheets. As a result of the conservatorship, however, Freddie Mac’s stock fell from $64 in January 2007 to $0.50 a share by January 2009 and Fannie Mae’s from $56.50 to $0.60, and both were trading below $1 (61 cents and 45 cents) when they were delisted from the New York Stock Exchange in June 2010.12

Releasing the GSEs from conservatorship will require a complex reorganization and several major decisions: how much capital each company must hold, whether to raise additional capital through an IPO, what role the government will continue to play in guaranteeing mortgages, and how to resolve Treasury’s senior preferred claim, estimated at $388 billion and still growing as retained profits are added to it.4 The GSE Exit Monitor aims to keep investors up to date on what could affect the restructuring and value of these securities: the latest policy announcements, prior-day closing prices, fair values and discounts under each outcome, updated institutional holdings and quarterly GSE earnings. Prices update each evening after the market close, so bookmark this page and check back often.

Back story: how we got here

The Great Financial Crisis (GFC) was triggered by the 2008 collapse of the housing market, driven by huge leverage and lax lending standards in subprime mortgage securities. Those subprime mortgages were held and financed by many Wall Street firms and banks, leading to a systemic meltdown of the global banking sector. The result was a number of prominent firms and banks either going out of business or being forced into mergers at severe discounts. To stem the contagion, the U.S. Government injected capital into a number of firms in return for equity stakes. Of note, these turned out to be good investments, and the workout took only a few short years. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, by contrast, have now been in conservatorship for 18 years.

  1. September 2008. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, two of the largest firms requiring capital, are put into conservatorship under the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA). Treasury commits $100 billion to each company (increased to $200 billion each in May 2009) to keep it solvent, in return for warrants (exercise price $0.00001 per share) for 79.9% of each company’s common stock, plus a 10% annual dividend, paid quarterly, and a liquidation preference equal to the initial $1 billion plus all amounts drawn. The investment is structured as senior preferred stock, ranking ahead of all other common and junior preferred stock. The liquidation preference is not reduced by any dividend payments and is required to be repaid. It stood at about $388 billion, and growing, as of October 2026.
  2. August 2012. Third Amendment to the Senior Preferred Stock Purchase Agreement. The 10% dividend is replaced by the “Net Worth Sweep,” which sends 100% of profits to the U.S. Government. The GSEs paid the government a total of $241.4 billion in dividends under the Net Worth Sweep from 2013 to 2019.
  3. September 2019. Under the Trump Administration, the sweep is capped: Fannie Mae may retain $25 billion of earnings and Freddie Mac $20 billion, with profits above those amounts still swept.
  4. January 2021. The sweep ends. Both companies may retain all their earnings to build the regulatory capital they need to exit conservatorship.

Under the 2019 and 2021 agreements, every dollar of net worth the companies retain is added to Treasury’s senior preferred liquidation preference. As a result, the GSEs’ own retained profits added $174.1 billion to the claim from 2019 through March 31, 2026 ($106.7 billion for Fannie Mae and $67.4 billion for Freddie Mac), and an estimated $189 billion through today. That is why a claim that started with the $191.4 billion Treasury invested now stands at about $388 billion.4

The conservator’s power

The FHFA assumed all powers of the board, management and common shareholders. Common and preferred stock dividends were immediately suspended.

Treasury backstop (SPSPAs)

The U.S. Department of the Treasury injected capital through Senior Preferred Stock Purchase Agreements (SPSPAs), ultimately investing $191.4 billion across both companies to ensure they remained solvent.

The “Net Worth Sweep”

From August 2012 until it was capped in 2019 and ended in 2021, the sweep sent 100% of profits to Treasury (see the timeline above).

Possible exit outcomes and timeline

One of the main reasons for the high price volatility, both up and down, in GSE securities is the uncertainty surrounding the timing and handling of the senior preferred securities held by the U.S. Government. If you are a current investor in the GSEs or plan to invest in these securities, you should stay current with news on this complex restructuring by signing up directly for policy announcements. Events could change at any time, and a clear announcement of likely resolution outcomes will create price volatility in the underlying securities. Investors should also be comfortable accepting the financial impact and potential losses if the bear case materializes.

Possible exit options

  1. Conversion. The entire senior preferred liquidation preference is converted to common stock.
    • Could raise additional capital through an IPO
    • Improves the balance sheet
    • Would cause severe dilution to existing shareholders and greatly reduce their value
  2. Forgiveness. Based on the argument that the government has already been fairly compensated, the liquidation preference is zeroed out.
    • The debt is settled and the senior preferred stock is canceled
    • Creates a clean balance sheet
    • A big win for existing common and junior preferred shareholders
  3. Hybrid. Some variation of options 1 and 2.
    • Treasury writes down a portion of the liquidation preference and converts some to common stock
    • Over a number of years, Treasury sells down some of its stock and warrant holdings through periodic public offerings

These possible exit options drive the valuation framework in the next section.

Valuation framework and restructuring projections

To evaluate potential upside and downside return profiles, consider the following four scenarios. The bull, base and bear values match the scenario columns in the fair value tables below.

1 · Bull case

Maximum upside

FNMA $32.64 · FMCC $41.64 · Junior preferred at a 7% yield

For comparative analysis, a fair value of $32.64 for FNMA common and $41.64 for FMCC common (12x average earnings per fully diluted share; see Fair value input variables) and a 7% yield for the junior preferred issues is used. This scenario assumes an optimal regulatory resolution: the senior preferred shares are completely forgiven, capital requirements are met, dividends are declared on the junior preferred, and the government’s 79.9% common stock warrants are fully exercised. This would pave the way for a clean exit from conservatorship and maximum equity upside. An IPO to raise additional capital could also occur at this time or shortly after.

2 · Base case

Target performance

FNMA $24.48 · FMCC $31.23 · Junior preferred at a 25% discount to fair value

For the baseline model, a 25% discount to fair value is used. This path assumes the senior preferred shares are not forgiven outright but are instead restructured or converted into common equity over an extended timeline. Junior preferred shareholders would be required to accept a 25% discount, either on par value or through a reverse stock split, resulting in minor dilution but establishing a predictable path toward recapitalization, resumed dividends and public market normalization. Investors can also take some comfort in the large institutional holders listed in Exhibit 2, who share the same interest in a negotiated, orderly exit.

3 · Downside case

Conservative projection

FNMA and FMCC common $10.00

For risk assessment, a below-average fair value of $10.00 for FNMA and FMCC common is used. This trajectory accounts for prolonged regulatory or legal friction. The senior preferred liquidation preference remains intact, consuming a significant portion of earnings through periodic commitment fees or dividends, which delays capital accumulation and suppresses the value of the common equity. In this scenario the common stock of both GSEs could be uplisted back to the NYSE, improving liquidity and institutional interest. The junior preferred would remain in limbo at a deep discount while waiting for further restructuring or a dividend on the common equity, which would trigger dividend payments on the junior preferred.

4 · Bear case

Severe downside

FNMA and FMCC $1.00 · Junior preferred $2.50

For stress testing, a fair value of $1.00 for both FNMA and FMCC common and $2.50 for the junior preferred is used. This worst case assumes the conservatorship continues indefinitely without structural change, common equity remains heavily diluted by senior obligations, and no value is realized from either forgiveness of the senior preferred or restructuring of the warrants. Investors must factor this outcome into any investment decision: the resolution has already taken more than 18 years, and a previous Democratic administration was essentially ready to wipe out common shareholders through the Net Worth Sweep. On the other hand, the Trump Administration attempted to recapitalize the GSEs during its first term, halted the Net Worth Sweep, and simply ran out of time.

Exit timeline

The exit timeline remains the great unknown, which is why investors need to stay current on GSE news flow and announcements. In an interview with the 2Way media platform reported on October 21, 2026, U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent signaled no near-term moves to end the conservatorship, but said, “I would imagine that the next year, the year after, we will be tackling the ownership structure,” pointing to 2027 or 2028.1

Other knowledgeable institutional investors have said they expect the Republican administration to want to finalize this process as a party victory, rather than leave it as a potential benefit or source of funds for a later Democratic administration. If that hypothesis is correct, an outcome could come within the next 12 to 18 months. Given the complexity of the restructuring, it could easily be a multi-phase transaction: resolution of the senior preferred, a dilution event, NYSE uplisting, and a subsequent IPO to raise capital.

This is the big issue to be resolved: how much of that $388 billion is really owed, and whether the onerous Net Worth Sweep was legal. Hence the lawsuits!

Investor note

The junior preferred securities were all issued as perpetual preferred securities, some twenty years ago. They have no stated maturity date, and all of them passed their optional call dates in previous years, so they can be called on short notice or on a set calendar schedule. If called, they must be redeemed at par value: $25 or $50, depending on the issue. The junior preferred rank senior to the common stock of both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, and junior to the U.S. Government’s senior preferred security.

If junior preferred dividends resume, the issues would be expected to be priced on yield. The dividend is paid on par value, not market price, so a 5% coupon on a $50 par value security pays $2.50 per share per year. If 7% were the going rate on average bank preferred securities, that issue’s market price would sit below par to reach a 7% yield (about $35.71, as shown in the Bull case column). The reverse is true for the high-yielding issues with coupons above 7%, FNMAS, FNMAT and FMCKJ, which would be expected to trade above par.

Coupon rates vary: some issues pay a fixed rate, while others float on a formula tied to a benchmark such as SOFR (which replaced LIBOR) or Treasury rates, plus or minus a spread. Verify the potential dividend amount before investing. Also keep in mind the difference between the $25 and $50 par values, and that the higher-coupon issues could be called at par at any time as part of the reorganization.

August 2020 CBO memo* (“Effects of Recapitalizing Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac Through Administrative Actions”). This memo was written six years ago and offered possible solutions for releasing the GSEs from conservatorship. Although it is dated, keep in mind that it was written during President Trump’s first term, and the same administration is in office today. It is a fascinating read and gives great detail on the GSE story up to that date.

In the section “Illustrative Options for Recapitalizing the GSEs Administratively,” CBO lists two hypothetical options for recapitalizing Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Under both options, the Treasury and FHFA would “alter the preferred-stock agreements to enable the GSEs to redeem the Treasury’s shares at their current face value,” a total of about $191 billion. CBO also suggested the GSEs could use a portion of IPO proceeds to “negotiate the repurchase of privately held junior preferred shares.”

CBO added alternatives: the Treasury could “delay redeeming its stake in the GSEs, perhaps by converting its senior preferred shares to common shares,” junior preferred shares or debt, or the GSEs could be placed into receivership and liquidated. More or less, it covers just about every option.

On the receivership route, CBO wrote:

“One option for receivership would be for FHFA to create a new corporation (or one for each GSE) and transfer to it all of the GSEs’ assets and liabilities, such as their mortgage guarantees. The new corporation could sell common stock to raise its required amount of capital. Any remaining proceeds from that sale could be allocated to the GSEs’ shareholders on the basis of their seniority—starting with the Treasury’s senior preferred shares before paying the private holders of junior preferred shares or common shares. The Treasury would be paid the same for its warrants as the existing holders of common shares.”

The key question is whether the current administration and decision-makers still support the approaches proposed in 2020. At a minimum, the memo shows the thinking at the time and the range of options considered for ending conservatorship. It also highlights the possible treatment and pecking order of the various securities.

July 2026 D.C. Circuit ruling: Fairholme Funds, Inc. v. FHFA (the Berkowitz case)

On July 24, 2026, a three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit (No. 25-5113) upheld the class-action verdict for Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac shareholders over the 2012 Net Worth Sweep. In 2023 a jury found that FHFA breached the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing owed to shareholders and awarded about $612.4 million; with prejudgment interest, the final judgment is about $812 million. Fairholme Funds, Bruce Berkowitz’s firm, is the lead plaintiff.

The court rejected all five of FHFA’s arguments: that the Supreme Court’s 2021 Collins v. Yellen decision barred the claim (it addressed only a statutory claim to stop the sweep, not a contract claim for damages); that the stock contracts left no room for the implied covenant; that the breach was only anticipatory; that shareholders failed to prove harm; and that investors who bought after the sweep was announced could not recover (the claims passed with the shares under Delaware and Virginia law). The court also upheld the denial of some plaintiffs’ bids for about $48 billion in restitution and for reliance damages.

FHFA, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac then asked the full D.C. Circuit to rehear the case; that request was reportedly denied in mid-October 2026. A Supreme Court petition is the remaining possible step. It is the first appeals court ruling upholding money damages to shareholders over the Net Worth Sweep, and many investors see it as a positive sign for the junior preferred and common. Sources: Orrick InfoBytes, Kessler Topaz, WRE News.

Investor note: If the case is ultimately successful and the $812 million is paid, the junior preferred could be eligible for an estimated payout of $0.50 to $0.75 per share held at the time of the final ruling. Eligibility, record dates and per-share amounts will be set by the court and class counsel; check the class notice before trading on this.
